# Research Pass 1 — Constructing a Strategy Quality Model

## 1. Quality is multi-object, not one score

A central error is to ask whether 'the strategy' was good without specifying what is being evaluated. At minimum, six objects should be separated: the quality of the strategic decision process; the quality of the formulated strategic argument/content; the quality of implementation; the quality of learning/adaptation; the realized outcome; and the quality of the later evaluation itself.

| **Object** | **Question** |
| --- | --- |
| Decision / process quality | Was the choice reasonable given information, alternatives, constraints and uncertainty available then? |
| Strategy-content quality | Did the formulated Strategy contain a defensible diagnosis/choice/logic without material internal contradiction? |
| Implementation quality | Were intended commitments/actions actually carried out with sufficient capability and coordination? |
| Adaptation quality | Was decision-relevant new evidence incorporated proportionately rather than ignored or overreacted to? |
| Outcome quality | Did the desired strategic results occur? |
| Evaluation quality | Are we judging without hindsight, survivorship and outcome contamination? |

## 2. Outcome is evidence, not identity

Strategic outcomes are usually jointly produced by choices, implementation, environmental conditions, competitor responses, timing and chance. Therefore outcome cannot be identical to Strategy quality. A poor result can update confidence in the strategic thesis without proving that the ex-ante decision was irrational; a favorable result can occur despite weak reasoning.

This distinction is reinforced by research on hindsight/outcome bias: once evaluators know what happened, they systematically reconstruct what should have been foreseeable. A mature evaluation must reconstruct the information set and uncertainty existing at the decision time.

## 3. Rumelt kernel is a strong normative candidate, not universal definition

Rumelt's diagnosis → guiding policy → coherent action structure has high decision value for deliberate problem-solving Strategy. It forces attention away from ambition and toward challenge, response and coordinated action. But Mintzberg & Waters show that realized Strategy may emerge without a fully corresponding prior intention. Therefore the kernel can be a strong quality architecture for formulated Strategy without becoming a necessary historical property of every realized Strategy.

## 4. Candidate minimum for deliberate/formulated Strategy

- A strategically consequential problem/opportunity representation sufficient to orient search and choice; it need not be a perfect 'root-cause diagnosis'.

- A consequential choice or commitment architecture that meaningfully constrains action/attention; a list of compatible aspirations is insufficient.

- A defensible means–ends logic at the level needed to explain why selected commitments could produce intended effects.

- No unrecognized material contradiction that destroys the Strategy's own intended logic.

- Feasibility sufficient to make the commitments more than rhetoric, while recognizing that feasibility itself may be built through Strategy.

- An epistemic posture that distinguishes evidence, assumptions and uncertainty and permits revision when decision-relevant evidence changes.

- Connection to action: Strategy need not be a detailed plan, but must be capable of governing consequential decisions.

## 5. These are not sufficient for success

Even a Strategy satisfying these conditions can fail in outcome because the causal model was wrong in an unknowable way, implementation failed, a competitor changed the game, a shock occurred, or expected-value reasoning produced an unfavorable realization. Quality criteria therefore cannot promise success; they improve criticizability and decision discipline.

## 6. Failure must be localized

| **Failure locus** | **Meaning** |
| --- | --- |
| Framing/Diagnosis failure | Material strategic situation was represented in a way that directed search toward the wrong problem/opportunity. |
| Choice failure | Commitment/trade-off was poor relative to credible alternatives and available information. |
| Causal-logic failure | Proposed mechanism did not work under relevant conditions. |
| Coherence failure | Material interactions among choices undermined intended logic. |
| Feasibility/capability failure | Strategy depended on capabilities/resources that could not be mobilized or built as assumed. |
| Implementation failure | Strategy was not executed as intended even though strategic thesis may remain plausible. |
| Adaptation failure | Material evidence arrived but the Strategy was not appropriately updated, or was over-updated from noise. |
| Outcome failure | Desired results were not achieved; locus remains undiagnosed. |
| Evaluation failure | Later observers misattribute luck, hindsight or execution to Strategy quality. |

## Pass 1 synthesis

Strategy quality should be evaluated as the defensibility of consequential strategic reasoning and commitments under the information and uncertainty available at the relevant time, plus the ability of that logic to govern action and be revised when material evidence changes. Outcome is an important updating signal, not the definition of quality.

# Research Pass 2 — Independent Adversarial Review

## Adversarial starting question

If the Pass 1 minimum is wrong, what successful or legitimate Strategies lack its proposed properties, and what apparently high-quality Strategies still fail?

## 1. Emergent Strategy attacks explicit-choice universality

Mintzberg & Waters make a decisive boundary visible: a realized strategic pattern may not correspond to explicit prior intention. Therefore explicit ex-ante choice, explicit diagnosis and explicit causal logic cannot be universal ontological requirements for all realized Strategy. They remain quality requirements for deliberate/formulated Strategy when an organization claims to have intentionally designed its course.

## 2. Strategy can be discovered before it can be explained

Organizations may discover a valuable pattern through experimentation, customer response or autonomous initiatives and only later articulate why it works. Requiring prior causal articulation would misclassify learning-based formation. The quality test must therefore distinguish formation from later strategic governance: once a pattern becomes consequential and intentionally retained, its causal assumptions and boundaries become legitimate objects of scrutiny.

## 3. Too much diagnosis can become analysis delay

Diagnosis has value only to the point that it changes strategic search or decision quality. Under uncertainty, action may itself generate the information required for diagnosis. Thus 'more complete diagnosis' is not monotonically better.

## 4. Coherence can overfit the present

RQ-09 already demonstrated that tight fit can increase reconfiguration costs and that modularity/tension can be rational. Therefore mutual reinforcement among all choices cannot be a universal quality criterion.

## 5. Process quality can become procedural absolution

A dangerous defense is: 'we followed a good process, therefore it was a good Strategy.' Repeatedly poor predictions or mechanisms should update confidence in the process itself. Ex-ante evaluation protects against hindsight bias but cannot immunize Strategy from accumulated ex-post evidence.

## 6. Outcome cannot be demoted too far

Although outcome is noisy, Strategy exists to affect outcomes. A theory of quality that remains permanently 'good' despite repeated failure becomes unfalsifiable. Outcomes should therefore update the strategic thesis through predicted intermediate states, alternative explanations and repeated evidence.

## 7. Implementation separation has limits

If a Strategy systematically requires an organization to execute capabilities it predictably lacks, calling subsequent failure 'only implementation' is evasive. Feasibility and capability assumptions belong partly inside Strategy quality.

## Pass 2 revised proposition

There is no defensible universal checklist requiring explicit diagnosis, explicit choice, explicit causal articulation and mutual reinforcement for every realized Strategy. The stronger claim is conditional: when Strategy is deliberately formulated and used to govern commitments, it should be criticizable through problem representation, consequential choice, means–ends reasoning, material interaction, feasibility and updating logic. Emergent Strategy can precede these articulations, but intentional retention makes them increasingly relevant.

# Research Pass 3 — Concept Destruction / Lock-in Audit

## Destructive starting question

Assume 'Strategy Quality' is a retrospective management fiction. Can the construct survive without circularity, outcome worship, or validation by our own previous chapters?

## 1. Remove-the-concept test

If 'good Strategy' is deleted, we can still ask sharper questions: Was the diagnosis decision-useful? Was the choice consequential? Were alternatives considered? Was the causal thesis defensible? Were contradictions recognized? Was execution feasible? Was new evidence incorporated? Did predicted intermediate effects occur? This shows that Strategy Quality is not one primitive property. It is an umbrella judgment over several distinct evaluative relations.

Surviving role: use 'quality' only as a summary after the underlying dimensions have been named.

## 2. Universal-core destruction

Attempting to produce a beautiful universal core creates architectural lock-in. The strongest counterexample is emergent Strategy: it can be real before explicit diagnosis, choice or causal argument exists. Therefore no large content checklist survives universally across both deliberate and emergent forms.

What survives at the broadest level is thinner: Strategy quality cannot be judged without reference to the relevant strategic object, time, information state, consequences and formation mode.

## 3. Rumelt self-validation test

RQ-07 diagnosis, RQ-08 causal logic and RQ-09 relational coherence resemble Rumelt's kernel. Using that resemblance as proof would be circular. Rumelt survives as a high-value normative problem-solving model, not as independent confirmation that our architecture is universally necessary.

## 4. Outcome-independence destruction

The opposite extreme — judging Strategy entirely independently of results — also fails. A causal thesis that repeatedly predicts intermediate changes that do not occur should lose credibility. Ex-ante quality and ex-post learning are distinct but connected. Good initial reasoning can later become an indefensible Strategy if evidence accumulates and adaptation fails.

## 5. Failure-rate mythology test

Popular statements that 50–90% of strategies fail cannot support a theory of Strategy quality. Cândido & Santos find the true implementation failure rate remains undetermined and that many published estimates rest on outdated, fragmentary, fragile or absent evidence. The concept of 'failure' is itself measured inconsistently.

## 6. Minimal counterexamples

| **Strong thesis** | **Minimal counterexample** | **Result** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Good Strategy always succeeds. | Sound expected-value choice meets adverse shock. | Destroyed. |
| Successful Strategy was good. | Weak thesis benefits from favorable external change. | Destroyed. |
| Good Strategy always starts with explicit diagnosis. | Valuable emergent pattern discovered through action. | Destroyed as universal. |
| Good Strategy requires explicit ex-ante causal model. | Pattern works before organization can explain it. | Destroyed as universal; retained for deliberate governance. |
| All strategic choices must reinforce one another. | Modular portfolio with limited interdependence. | Destroyed. |
| Implementation failure is never Strategy failure. | Strategy predictably depends on unavailable capability. | Destroyed. |
| Good process absolves poor outcomes indefinitely. | Repeated mechanism failure without revision. | Destroyed. |
| Outcome alone proves bad Strategy. | Outcome dominated by luck/shock. | Destroyed. |

## 7. Decision-usefulness test

The quality construct survives only if it changes evaluation and future decisions. It does so when it prevents outcome bias, localizes failure, identifies whether a thesis deserves continuation, distinguishes execution correction from Strategy Review, and exposes rhetoric that cannot govern consequential decisions.

## Strongest surviving claims after Pass 3

- Strategy Quality is not one primitive scalar property; it is a scoped judgment over distinct dimensions.

- Outcome quality and Strategy quality are related but non-identical.

- Ex-ante evaluation should reconstruct the information, alternatives and uncertainty available at the decision time.

- Ex-post evidence must still update the strategic thesis; ex-ante reasonableness is not permanent immunity.

- For deliberate/formulated Strategy, decision-useful problem representation, consequential choice/commitment, defensible causal reasoning, absence of destructive unrecognized contradictions, feasibility and revision logic are strong quality requirements.

- For emergent Strategy, explicit prior articulation cannot be required; formation quality and later intentional governance must be separated.

- Failure should be localized before it is attributed to Strategy as a whole.

- Implementation and Strategy are distinguishable but not cleanly separable when feasibility/capability assumptions were strategically foreseeable.

- No universal failure-rate statistic is currently justified by the reviewed implementation literature.

- Any final minimum definition in Part 01 must be thinner than a full normative checklist.

# Failure Taxonomy — Working Synthesis

Failure should be diagnosed as a chain rather than a label:

Situation representation → Strategic choice → Causal thesis → Configuration/feasibility → Implementation → Adaptation → Outcome → Evaluation

A break at one point can propagate forward, but downstream failure does not automatically identify the upstream cause.

# Founder / Learner Evaluation Questions

- What exactly am I evaluating: decision, Strategy content, execution, adaptation or outcome?

- What information and alternatives were genuinely available when the commitment was made?

- What strategic problem/opportunity did the Strategy make decision-relevant?

- What consequential choice did it create, and what did it constrain or refuse?

- What causal thesis linked commitment to intended outcome?

- What evidence would have weakened that thesis before the outcome was known?

- Were material contradictions/dependencies visible and governed?

- Were capability and feasibility assumptions plausible rather than aspirational?

- Was the Strategy enacted, or am I evaluating a document that never became behavior?

- What did later evidence change, and did the organization update proportionately?

- How much of the outcome is diagnostic of the Strategy versus execution, environment and chance?

- Where exactly did failure occur, and what competing explanation remains?

# Final Educational Synthesis

RQ-11 does not produce a universal recipe for a 'good Strategy'. Three passes make that conclusion too strong. Instead, it produces a disciplined architecture for judging Strategy without confusing it with ambition, execution or outcome.

At the broadest level, Strategy quality is contextual: the evaluator must specify the strategic object, formation mode, time horizon and information state. For deliberately formulated Strategy, stronger requirements survive: a decision-useful representation of the strategic situation; consequential choice/commitment; defensible causal reasoning; recognition of material contradictions and dependencies; plausible feasibility; connection to action; and an updating logic for material evidence.

These requirements are not sufficient for success. Strategy operates under uncertainty, and outcome is jointly produced by strategy, execution, environment and chance. But the opposite escape also fails: an initially reasonable Strategy cannot remain 'good' forever while its causal predictions repeatedly fail. Ex-ante quality and ex-post learning must be connected.

Failure is therefore not one label. It must be localized across diagnosis, choice, causal logic, coherence, feasibility, implementation, adaptation, outcome and evaluation. Only after localization is it legitimate to say that Strategy itself failed.

The strongest meta-conclusion for Part 01 is intentionally thin: Strategy should be judged by whether it creates a defensible and consequential logic for governing action under constraints and uncertainty, appropriate to its formation mode, and whether that logic remains open to material evidence. The exact final definition must now be tested retrospectively against RQ-01–RQ-10 rather than declared here.
