## Для чего существует эта глава

Стратегия может быть логически последовательной, хорошо оформленной и всё равно ошибочной, если она отвечает не на тот вопрос. RQ-07 исследует интеллектуальную работу до выбора решения: как из сложной, неоднозначной ситуации получить достаточно хорошее стратегическое представление о starting point — что происходит, почему это существенно, где находится центральный challenge или opportunity, что является симптомом, причиной, constraint, interpretation или неизвестным.

Цель главы — научить не «заполнять SWOT», а формировать и проверять strategic diagnosis. Диагноз должен направлять поиск и выбор, но не маскировать предположения под факты и не превращаться в преждевременное решение. Хорошая диагностика не обязана описывать всю реальность; она должна выделять те аспекты реальности, от которых действительно зависит стратегический выбор.

## AI Research & Review Annotation

Research task: determine what strategic diagnosis/problem formulation is, why it matters, what a defensible starting-point representation contains, and how framing changes subsequent search and choice. Do not assume Rumelt's diagnosis is a universal ontology; do not equate diagnosis with data collection, SWOT, root-cause analysis, issue labeling, or a single objectively discoverable “true problem”. Test strategic-issue diagnosis, problem-formulation, cognition/framing, problem-solving, behavioral decision, search and renewal traditions.

Incoming context: RQ-01–RQ-06 leave open the threshold of strategicness, Agency ↔ Emergence, and the role of problem framing before choice. RQ-06 explicitly requires a minimal framing check before opportunity evaluation. RQ-07 owns deep diagnosis/problem formulation but must not pre-empt RQ-08 Causal Logic, RQ-09 Coherence, or RQ-10 Adaptation. Pass 2 must begin by asking which strongest theory or evidence would show that Pass-1 diagnosis model is wrong, overly rational, overly objective, or too problem-centric.

# Layer 0 — Chapter Passport & AI Research Specification

## 1. Place in Curriculum

RQ-07 follows Strategic Go / No-Go and begins the next logical movement: before an organization can evaluate alternatives reliably, it must decide what situation it believes it is facing. Как организация должна формировать и проверять strategic diagnosis / starting point, чтобы её Strategy отвечала на реальный challenge или opportunity, а не на симптом, удобный narrative или преждевременно выбранное решение?

# 3. Research Pass 1 — Building the Diagnosis Model

## 3.1 Diagnosis is selective representation, not data inventory

Rumelt’s normative contribution is useful because it treats diagnosis as an explanation that simplifies an overwhelming situation by identifying critical aspects. Dutton, Fahey and Narayanan provide a complementary process view: strategic issue diagnosis is an interpretive process with inputs, process characteristics and outputs. Together they suggest that diagnosis is neither raw data nor a list of observations. It is a selective representation intended to make subsequent strategic reasoning possible.

The selection is unavoidable. Managers cannot model every variable, stakeholder, causal path and uncertainty. Gavetti and Levinthal show that cognitive representations can guide search precisely because they reduce a high-dimensional landscape to a simpler representation. The benefit and danger are the same: simplification directs attention and search, but may exclude valuable regions of the problem space.

Working distinction: a starting-point description says what is observed; a diagnosis says what aspects of the situation are strategically critical and how they are interpreted. A causal model goes further by specifying mechanisms. RQ-07 may contain causal hypotheses, but RQ-08 owns the deeper test of causality.

## 3.2 Facts, signals, interpretations and hypotheses must not collapse

| **Element** | **Working meaning** | **Typical error** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Observation / data | Recorded state, event or measurement. | Treating measurement as explanation. |
| Signal | Observation considered potentially meaningful. | Assuming salience equals strategic importance. |
| Interpretation | Meaning assigned to observations. | Presenting interpretation as fact. |
| Issue | Condition brought to organizational attention as requiring consideration. | Assuming every issue is a problem. |
| Symptom | Observed manifestation that may result from deeper mechanisms. | Solving the visible symptom. |
| Problem | A framed discrepancy/obstacle/tension requiring response or search. | Assuming framing is neutral. |
| Opportunity | A framed possibility for desirable value/outcome under conditions. | Equating upside with evidence. |
| Constraint | Condition limiting feasible action. | Treating inherited assumption as fixed constraint. |
| Cause hypothesis | Proposed mechanism producing relevant state. | Calling correlation a root cause. |
| Diagnosis | Selective, evidence-informed interpretation of the strategically material situation. | Turning preferred solution into diagnosis. |
| Unknown | Material question whose answer is not currently established. | Filling gap with confident narrative. |

This registry is intentionally provisional. Several concepts are owned more deeply by later chapters; RQ-07 needs only enough precision to prevent category errors.

## 3.3 Problem formulation changes solution search

Baer, Dirks and Nickerson make the strongest direct claim for this chapter: before a strategy can be developed, the problem it addresses must be formulated. Their theory focuses on complex, ill-structured problems and shows how heterogeneous teams can face impediments that narrow problem formulation and therefore constrain solution search and potential value creation. Nickerson and Zenger likewise use the problem as a unit of analysis and argue that problem complexity should influence how solution search is organized.

The mechanism is important: formulation establishes boundaries and decomposition of the problem; these determine which knowledge is considered relevant, which alternatives are generated, and which actors are invited into search. A narrow formulation can make a high-quality solution impossible before evaluation begins.

However, Transfer Validity is limited: Nickerson & Zenger develop a theory of firm/governance for problem solving, and Baer et al. focus on strategic problem formulation in heterogeneous teams. We can transfer the search-space mechanism to strategic diagnosis, but not claim their organizational prescriptions are universal Strategy theory.

## 3.4 Problem, symptom, cause, constraint, opportunity, desired state

A frequent strategic error is to name a desired state as the problem: “we need growth”, “we need digitalization”, “we need market leadership”. These are aspirations or solution directions, not diagnoses. Another is to treat a symptom — falling sales, employee turnover, slow delivery — as the problem without testing competing explanations. Conversely, an organization can over-invest in root-cause analysis when the strategically relevant issue is not the deepest historical cause but the leverageable mechanism that can be changed.

Therefore RQ-07 distinguishes ontological depth from strategic usefulness. The deepest cause is not automatically the best strategic target. Strategy needs an explanation sufficient to identify consequential constraints, mechanisms and leverage possibilities. Full causal discipline is deferred to RQ-08.

## 3.5 Opportunity and threat are frames, not neutral properties

Dutton & Jackson’s work on categorizing strategic issues argues that labeling an issue as threat or opportunity affects information processing, motivation and organizational response. Jackson & Dutton found evidence of threat bias: managers can be more sensitive to characteristics associated with threats. Thus “opportunity” and “threat” are not merely objective labels attached after analysis; they can shape the analysis itself.

This matters directly for RQ-06. An opportunity entering Go/No-Go has already been framed as an opportunity. RQ-07 therefore adds a precondition: ask what alternative frame would make the same evidence look like a threat, constraint, symptom or signal of a different underlying change.

## 3.6 Cognitive simplification is necessary and dangerous

Schwenk reviews cognitive simplification processes affecting goal formulation, problem identification, alternative generation and evaluation under uncertainty. Walsh similarly emphasizes that knowledge structures help process information but can limit understanding. The strategic implication is not “avoid mental models” — impossible — but make them visible enough to challenge.

A useful diagnosis therefore has two qualities in tension: compression and contestability. It reduces complexity enough to guide attention, while preserving the assumptions and excluded alternatives that would allow another frame to challenge it.

## 3.7 Diagnosis is socially and politically produced

Kaplan’s framing-contests model provides a strong corrective to purely rational diagnosis. Under uncertainty, actors hold different cognitive frames about market direction and appropriate solutions; they engage in political framing practices to make their frame resonate and mobilize action. The prevailing diagnosis can therefore be both an interpretation of evidence and an outcome of influence.

This does not make diagnosis arbitrary. It means quality control cannot rely on consensus alone. A robust process should distinguish evidential strength from organizational support and create legitimate space for competing frames, especially where the decision is consequential and uncertain.

## 3.8 Starting point includes the organization, not only the environment

Strategic diagnosis cannot be only external scanning. The same environmental change has different implications for organizations with different capabilities, commitments, positions, legitimacy and resource constraints. Tripsas & Gavetti’s Polaroid case shows how managerial cognition and accumulated capabilities jointly shape adaptation to technological change. Barr, Stimpert & Huff similarly find renewal depends on linking environmental change to corporate strategy and modifying that linkage over time.

Thus the strategic starting point is relational: environment × organization × commitments × interpretation. This strengthens RQ-03’s object logic and prevents diagnosis from becoming a generic market report.

## 3.9 Competing diagnoses are often better than premature consensus

Because complex strategic situations can support multiple plausible frames, an early single diagnosis risks confirmation bias. A disciplined process should generate at least the strongest competing explanation when stakes and uncertainty justify it. The purpose is not endless debate but falsification: what evidence would make our preferred frame materially weaker?

Competing diagnoses are particularly valuable when different frames imply different strategic choices. If all frames lead to the same action, further diagnostic discrimination may have low decision value.

## 3.10 When is diagnosis sufficient?

Perfect diagnosis is impossible and often undesirable because information has cost and environments change. A diagnosis is sufficient when it discriminates among materially different strategic responses well enough to justify the next commitment or learning action. This creates a decision-value stopping rule rather than a completeness rule.

The test is counterfactual: which unresolved uncertainty, if answered differently, would change our choice, commitment size or Go/No-Go state? If none, more diagnosis may be analysis delay. If a low-cost inquiry could reverse a consequential irreversible choice, stopping is premature.

# 4. Pass-1 Working Synthesis

A strategic diagnosis is a selective, evidence-informed and revisable representation of the organization-in-environment situation that identifies the strategically material challenge/opportunity, distinguishes observations from interpretations and unknowns, and narrows subsequent search without pretending to describe reality completely.

## Pass-1 working sequence:

Signals / observations → competing interpretations → issue/problem/opportunity framing → boundaries & assumptions → provisional mechanism hypotheses → strategically material challenge → implications for search/choice → tests / unknowns

This is a pedagogical synthesis, not an established universal process. Pass 2 must attack its rationality, objectivity, sequence and problem-centric assumptions.

# 5. Research Pass 2 — Independent Adversarial Re-run

Independence question: If the Pass-1 model is wrong, what strongest theory/evidence would show that strategic diagnosis is not a clean, evidence-led representation formed before choice?

## 5.1 Countermodel: diagnosis may emerge through action

Mintzberg, Raisinghani & Théoret’s empirical work on unstructured strategic decisions undermines a clean sequence of diagnose → choose → act. Identification, development and selection can cycle and interrupt one another. Gavetti & Levinthal likewise distinguish forward-looking cognitive search from backward-looking experiential search. Action can therefore generate the experience required to revise the representation.

Revision: H1 is strengthened for formulated reasoning but weakened as a strict temporal sequence. Diagnosis is logically prior to defending a choice, but empirically it can be iteratively co-produced with search, experimentation and action.

## 5.2 Countermodel: “true problem” may be an illusion

Framing and cognition research shows that ambiguous situations can support multiple internally coherent representations. Kaplan demonstrates that frames are shaped through interaction and political contest. Starbuck & Milliken warn against retrospective stories that make past perception appear more accurate and linear than it was. This attacks any notion that diagnosis simply discovers one hidden objective problem.

Revision: H2 strengthened. Diagnostic quality should be judged by evidence, discriminating usefulness, explanatory adequacy, contestability and revisability — not by claiming access to a unique final truth.

## 5.3 Countermodel: reframing is not always superior

Nutt’s empirical studies report that reframing can perform well and that quantitative performance-gap diagnostics can also produce strong decision outcomes. This prevents the chapter from romanticizing broad reframing. In structured, measurable situations, established diagnostics and clear gaps may be more efficient than opening the problem space indefinitely.

Revision: diagnosis method should match problem structure and decision stakes. “Always reframe” becomes an antipattern of its own.

## 5.4 Countermodel: cognition can lag despite evidence

Tripsas & Gavetti show that managerial beliefs can constrain adaptive response even while organizational capabilities evolve. Barr et al. show renewal depends on updating the linkage between environmental change and strategy. More data therefore does not guarantee better diagnosis if the governing mental model filters or reinterprets it.

Revision: evidence collection must be paired with frame challenge. A diagnostic process should ask what evidence the current model systematically makes hard to notice.

## 5.5 Countermodel: labels create action, not just description

Threat/opportunity categorization research implies that diagnosis is performative: labels influence attention, motivation and response. Therefore diagnosis can change the organization while supposedly describing the situation.

Revision: diagnosis should disclose consequential labels and, where possible, test alternative neutral formulations before assigning threat/opportunity language.

## 5.6 Countermodel: politics can select a compelling but weak diagnosis

Kaplan’s framing contests show that political skill can shape which frame prevails. Consensus, executive sponsorship or narrative resonance cannot therefore serve as evidence quality proxies.

Revision: for high-consequence issues, the process needs a dissent/counter-frame mechanism and a record of evidence that distinguishes “accepted” from “well supported”.

## 5.7 Countermodel: problem framing can become solution smuggling

Problem statements often embed a preferred remedy: “How do we digitize?”, “How do we enter market X?”, “How do we cut cost by 20%?” These formulations close alternatives before diagnosis. Baer et al.’s concern with narrowed formulation makes this especially important.

Revision: diagnosis should be tested for solution content. If removing the proposed solution changes the supposed problem, the frame may be prematurely prescriptive.

## 5.8 Revised synthesis after Pass 2

Diagnosis is not a one-time discovery phase. It is a disciplined, revisable framing process that compresses complexity enough to guide search while remaining open to counter-frames, evidence and learning from action. Its output is not “truth”, but a sufficiently defensible representation for the next strategic commitment.

Revised loop: observe → frame → compare frames → search/test → update representation → choose/act at bounded scale → learn → re-diagnose when triggers invalidate the frame.

# 12. Antipatterns & Failure Modes

| --- | --- | --- |

| Solution smuggling | Preferred action is embedded in problem statement. | Alternatives disappear before search. |
| Framework substitution | SWOT/PESTEL/etc. output substitutes for judgment. | Information without diagnosis. |

| Root-cause fetish | Deepest historical cause is pursued regardless of leverage. | Strategically irrelevant analysis. |

| Permanent diagnosis | Initial frame survives environmental change. | Cognitive inertia / strategic drift. |

| Hindsight diagnosis | Past outcome is used to reconstruct an apparently obvious problem. | False learning and overconfidence. |

# 13. Strategic Diagnosis Questions

1. What exactly have we observed, and what have we inferred?

2. What is signal, symptom, problem, cause hypothesis, constraint and desired state?

3. Whose strategic object is being diagnosed?

4. What changed — environment, organization, relationship between them, or our interpretation?

5. What problem statement would a person who rejects our preferred solution use?

6. What is the strongest competing diagnosis?

7. Which evidence discriminates between the competing frames?

8. What important evidence does our current frame make easy to ignore?

9. Are we labeling the issue as threat/opportunity too early?

10. Which assumptions are carrying the diagnosis?

11. What material unknown could change the strategic choice?

12. Are we seeking the deepest cause or the strategically actionable mechanism?

13. Does the diagnosis narrow search usefully without closing plausible alternatives prematurely?

14. What would falsify or materially weaken this diagnosis?

15. When is the diagnosis sufficient for the next bounded commitment?

16. What event/evidence should force re-diagnosis later?

# 14. Application Note — deliberately limited

For a real company, RQ-07 should not yet answer “what is our strategic problem?”. It supplies the discipline for discovering it. Before evaluating a market, project, partnership or investment through RQ-06, the team should produce a compact starting-point representation that separates evidence from interpretation, states at least one competing frame where stakes justify it, and names the unknowns capable of changing the decision. Existing company ideas remain examples to test the method, not evidence that the diagnosis is correct.

# 21. Final Synthesis

RQ-07 rejects two extremes. Strategic diagnosis is neither objective discovery of one hidden “true problem” nor arbitrary storytelling. It is a selective, evidence-informed and revisable representation built to make consequential strategic search and choice possible. Its quality depends on what it makes visible, what it excludes, how clearly it separates observations from interpretations and unknowns, whether competing frames can challenge it, and whether remaining uncertainty matters to the next decision.

The chapter also changes the meaning of “starting point”. It is not a dashboard snapshot. It is the current relationship among environment, organization, inherited commitments, capabilities and the interpretive frame through which decision-makers understand them. Because frames guide search, a wrong or stale representation can make later choice sophisticated but irrelevant.

For formulated Strategy, diagnosis is therefore logically prior to justification of a guiding choice, but not necessarily temporally first. Action, experiments and experience can update diagnosis. The appropriate standard is not completeness but decision-relevant sufficiency: enough understanding to justify the next bounded commitment while preserving explicit triggers for re-diagnosis.

The next chapter, RQ-08 Causal Logic, receives a deliberately constrained handoff: RQ-07 identifies cause hypotheses and strategically material mechanisms, but it has not proven causality. RQ-08 must now determine what kind of causal reasoning a Strategy actually needs and how to distinguish causal logic from persuasive story.
